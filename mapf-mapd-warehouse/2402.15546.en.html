Multi-Agent PathfindingMAPF
In short: HiMAP is a scalable approach for large-scale multi-agent pathfinding that uses imitation learning with heuristic guidance to achieve competitive performance.

What is this paper about?

Multi-Agent Pathfinding (MAPF) involves finding collision-free paths for multiple agents while minimizing metrics like makespan or flowtime. As the number of agents increases, traditional centralized algorithms struggle with scalability. While Reinforcement Learning (RL) offers a decentralized alternative, it often suffers from complex implementation, long training times, and unstable convergence.

What is new compared with prior work?

The authors propose HiMAP, a novel method that formulates MAPF as an imitation learning (IL) problem using heuristic expert solutions. They introduce a simple training scheme based on small-scale expert paths and propose several inference techniques to enhance performance. The work demonstrates that imitation-learning-only MAPF can be effective when equipped with specific inference strategies.

What is the key technical idea?

HiMAP utilizes a neural network architecture similar to PRIMAL but without value or blocking output heads. It learns to imitate the action probability distributions of a heuristic teacher. During inference, the model employs three key techniques: Preventing Re-Visit (using a history of 5 time steps), Treating Completed Agents as Obstacles, and Softmax Temperature Adaptation to improve decision-making quality.

How was it validated?

The method was validated on 1,000 scenarios across 200 random square maps with varying agent counts (4, 8, 16, 32, and 64). Expert paths were generated using the EECBS algorithm. The results show that HiMAP achieves competitive success rates and scalability within the imitation-learning-only paradigm, with ablation studies confirming the necessity of the proposed inference techniques.

What are the limitations and open questions?

Unlike prior imitation learning methods that rely on single-agent shortest paths, HiMAP uses collision-free paths from EECBS, allowing agents to account for others' behaviors. However, the authors note that HiMAP does not yet outperform state-of-the-art learning-based methods. Future work could involve larger datasets, hybridizing domain knowledge, or incorporating communication between agents.

Detailed section summary

Background

MAPF is a critical problem in automated systems like warehouse robotics, aviation, and gaming. Traditional approaches include reducing the problem to Integer Linear Programming (ILP) or Satisfiability (SAT), or using search-based algorithms like Conflict-Based Search (CBS).

Limitations of prior work

The primary challenge in MAPF is scalability; as agent density and numbers increase, centralized planning becomes computationally prohibitive. Additionally, existing RL-based decentralized solvers often require extensive tuning and suffer from training instability.

Technical highlights

The model processes agent observations through four distinct channels. To avoid conflicts during sampling, simple rules are applied to handle swapping or vertex conflicts by recursively recovering agent states.

Experiments

Experiments were conducted on 100 maps with 0.3 obstacle density and another 100 maps with the same density. Training was performed on a single NVIDIA TITAN X GPU for approximately 100 minutes using a learning rate that decays every eight epochs.

Results

HiMAP demonstrates high success rates in environments with high obstacle density. Ablation studies specifically show that omitting the 'Preventing Re-Visit' or 'Treating Completed Agents as Obstacles' features significantly reduces performance.

Conclusion

HiMAP provides a simple, low-cost, and scalable imitation learning framework for MAPF. By leveraging heuristic guidance and specialized inference techniques, it achieves competitive results, proving the potential of pure imitation learning in complex multi-agent coordination tasks.

Limitations and open problems

The study acknowledges that HiMAP currently falls short of the performance of state-of-the-art learning-based techniques. The training data is limited to relatively small-scale problems, which may limit the model's ability to generalize to even larger scenarios.

Why it matters to MAPF researchers

This work is important because it simplifies the training process for MAPF solvers by replacing complex RL with straightforward imitation learning, while maintaining scalability through effective inference-time adjustments.

Who should read this paper

Researchers working on multi-agent coordination, scalability in pathfinding, and practitioners looking for efficient, decentralized training methods for autonomous agents.
