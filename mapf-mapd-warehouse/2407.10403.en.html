In short: This paper addresses the difficulty of obtaining cooperative behavior in distributed, partially observed multi-agent pathfinding when agents are trained with Independent Q-Learning. It proposes CoRS, which incorporates an agent’s influence on nearby agents into its reward, and reports improved performance over DHC especially in settings with many agents.

What is this paper about?

The task is multi-agent pathfinding on grid environments, where multiple agents must reach their goals while avoiding obstacles, other agents, and path conflicts. The motivating applications include ports, airports, and warehouses containing many mobile robots. Centralized planners can use global information to produce efficient paths, but their computational demand and planning time can grow as the number of agents increases. Decentralized planners scale better to large systems, yet local information can lead to insufficient cooperation, lower success rates, and reduced overall efficiency. The paper frames this tension as a suitable target for multi-agent reinforcement learning.

What is new compared with prior work?

The main contribution is CoRS, a cooperative reward-shaping technique built around Independent Q-Learning. It evaluates how one agent affects neighboring agents and incorporates that interaction into the reward, encouraging behavior that considers more than the agent’s own immediate objective. Cooperation is introduced during training while execution remains distributed, distinguishing the approach from methods that depend on elaborate communication mechanisms. The paper combines CoRS with DHC, calling the resulting system CoRS-DHC, and compares it with DHC and other state-of-the-art planners. What is the key technical idea?

MAPF is modeled as a partially observed multi-agent reinforcement-learning problem in which each agent selects actions from local observations. The environment uses four-neighbor grid movement; agents have a field of view and can communicate with up to two nearest neighbors during the reported experiments. The base reward gives different values to movement toward or away from the goal, staying on or off the goal, collisions, and task completion. The reported values are minus 0.070 for moving toward the goal, minus 0.075 for moving away, 0 for staying on the goal, minus 0.075 for staying off the goal, minus 0.5 for a collision, and 3 for finishing. CoRS modifies the reward to account for neighborhood influence, aiming to discourage blocking or colliding with other agents. The method uses the DHC network structure and fine-tunes a previously obtained optimal policy network rather than training the network entirely from the initial state.

Background

MAPF seeks collision-free routes for multiple agents and is commonly applied to grid abstractions of multi-robot environments. The paper distinguishes centralized algorithms, which exploit global information, from decentralized algorithms, which prioritize scalability and local decision making. MARL can learn distributed policies from partial observations and is therefore attractive for large agent populations. Centralized-training distributed-execution methods can use global information during training, while distributed-training distributed-execution methods lack that information and may encourage agents to maximize only their individual rewards. Limitations of prior work

Independent Q-Learning is scalable to many agents but often performs poorly when the task requires strong coordination. In MAPF, an action that is locally beneficial can block another agent’s route or create a collision, even if it improves the acting agent’s own reward. This mismatch between individual optimization and system-level traffic motivates a reward that reflects neighborhood effects. CoRS is intended to reduce this mismatch without requiring centralized execution or a complex communication policy.

Technical highlights

CoRS measures or represents the effect of an agent on its neighbors and uses that interaction in reward shaping.
