In short: This paper treats collision avoidance and task assignment for automated guided vehicles in a robotic mobile fulfillment system as multi-objective problems involving travel time and energy consumption. It proposes a modified A* routing procedure, an energy-aware collision-priority rule, and NSGA-II and ALNS task-assignment methods; the reported computational experiments show better Pareto-front indicators than the stated benchmarks.

What is this paper about?

The setting is a robotic mobile fulfillment system in which multiple automated guided vehicles transport pods between storage locations and workstations. The vehicles must be assigned tasks and routed through a parallel-aisle warehouse while avoiding head-on, crossing, and stay-on conflicts, as well as possible deadlocks in narrow aisles. Much prior MAPF work emphasizes collision reduction and travel time, whereas this study adds energy consumption associated with the vehicles’ loading status. The evidence describes a hypothetical RMFS rather than an implementation validated in a physical warehouse.

What is new compared with prior work?

The first contribution is a collision-avoidance strategy that uses travel time and energy consumption when establishing priorities between conflicting AGVs. The second is a multi-objective task-assignment framework implemented with an NSGA-II approach and a modified Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search procedure, with objectives that include time, energy, collision risk, and travel distance. The work also introduces an ARC operator intended to balance the number of tasks assigned to different AGVs while searching the solution space. What is the key technical idea?

The problem is formulated with sets and variables for AGVs, locations, workstations, task locations, loading status, time, and movements on a space-time network. Its four objectives are total running time, maximum running time, total energy consumption, and maximum energy consumption across the AGVs. Routing uses a modified A* search that evaluates location-time states rather than only spatial distance, and collision handling updates the path time when an AGV must wait, step back, or take an alternative route. Task assignment is searched through NSGA-II and ALNS, with multi-objective dominance, neighborhood generation, operator-weight adaptation, and solution acceptance incorporated into the heuristic process. The collision-priority procedure attempts to resolve conflicts and identify deadlocks when vehicles meet inside an aisle.

Detailed section summary

Background

The paper motivates the problem through the growth of e-commerce and the importance of timely order fulfillment. In a robotic mobile fulfillment system, AGVs move storage pods to workstations, allowing the system to adjust paths dynamically instead of requiring workers to travel throughout the warehouse. Operational decisions include task allocation, path planning, and collision-priority rules, in addition to higher-level decisions such as storage-area sizing and workstation placement. This study focuses on the operational level.

Limitations of prior work

Several AGVs can compete for the same aisle or intersection, creating head-on, crossing, or stay-on conflicts. A conflict-resolution rule that minimizes only delay may overlook the energy consequences of moving a loaded or unloaded vehicle. The paper therefore models routing and task allocation with both aggregate and worst-case travel-time and energy objectives. It seeks feasible, non-conflicting routes and task assignments while preserving a useful set of trade-off solutions rather than a single scalar optimum.

Technical highlights

The mathematical formulation represents task assignment, task order, pod pickup and return, loading status, and vehicle movement through a time-expanded network. The four objective functions are total travel time, maximum vehicle travel time, total energy consumption, and maximum vehicle energy consumption. The modified A* procedure evaluates candidate position-time combinations and carries collision-related lost time into subsequent states. Waiting, moving away or stepping back, and rerouting are treated as possible responses to conflicts; a priority rule is used when aisle interactions can otherwise create a deadlock. The task-assignment heuristics use Pareto dominance instead of reducing all objectives to one stated scalar score.

The priority-rule experiment uses thirteen scenarios with different numbers of tasks and AGVs and compares average Pareto-front indicators for the benchmark and proposed routing-priority combinations. The task-assignment experiment evaluates ALNS and NSGA-II in proposed and benchmark forms for cases involving five, eight, and ten AGVs. Conclusion

The paper extends RMFS path planning and MAPF-style operational planning by treating energy consumption as a first-class objective alongside travel time. Its modified A* routing, energy-aware collision-priority rule, and multi-objective NSGA-II and ALNS task-assignment methods are reported to outperform the benchmarks on computational Pareto indicators for the hypothetical scenarios. The authors especially emphasize the proposed methods’ task-assignment performance and the stronger results obtained with NSGA-II. Why it matters to MAPF researchers
