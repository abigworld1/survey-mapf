What is this paper about?

Classical Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF) computes collision-free paths for multiple agents in a shared environment while assuming that static obstacles are known accurately in advance. In real deployments, a fallen object, a closed passage, or a spill can make locations inaccessible and invalidate a plan. This paper considers map discrepancies that are unknown before execution but fixed once execution begins, with agents discovering them through local observations. The setting is especially concerned with spatially correlated uncertainty, where observing one location changes the estimated traversability of nearby locations that have not yet been observed. The objective is therefore not only to resolve conflicts among agents, but also to select routes that account for uncertain future availability.

What is new compared with prior work?

Prior approaches to uncertain MAPF either construct contingent plans branching on future observations or interleave execution with replanning after direct observations. The paper argues that these approaches do not use spatial dependence to infer the state of nearby unobserved locations, so they may discover a hidden obstacle only after an expensive rerouting decision becomes necessary. It introduces Multi-Agent Gaussian belief Inference for Coordination, or MAGIC, as a framework for maintaining and sharing a spatial belief over traversability during execution. MAGIC represents correlations with a Gaussian Markov Random Field and approximately updates the belief with Gaussian Belief Propagation. It then exposes uncertainty to existing MAPF planners through traversal costs that account for the likely consequences of using a location and needing an alternative route. Thus, the claimed novelty is an integration of spatial probabilistic inference with scalable MAPF planning and explicit collision resolution rather than a replacement for every underlying planner.

What is the key technical idea?

MAGIC repeatedly solves a deterministic, weighted MAPF problem using the observations collected so far instead of directly optimizing the full decision problem over hidden states and future observation histories. First, it infers a shared posterior belief about the traversability of locations that remain unobserved. The Gaussian Markov Random Field uses a prior-anchoring term and a penalty on differences between adjacent latent scores, thereby encoding spatial correlation. Gaussian Belief Propagation approximately propagates the effect of new agent observations through that field. The resulting beliefs are translated into detour-aware traversal costs, so a route through an uncertain location can be weighed against the cost of recovering if that location is blocked. Agents execute the current plan until new observations trigger another belief update and planning update. Under the stated observation model, adjacent uncertain destinations are observed before entry and observations are noiseless; consequently, a sound planner produces actions that are valid in the realized environment.

Detailed section summary

Background

MAPF is commonly modeled on an undirected graph whose vertices are locations and whose edges are allowed moves. Each agent has a start and a goal, time is discretized, and agents may wait or move to adjacent locations while avoiding vertex and edge conflicts. MAPF under obstacle uncertainty, referred to in the excerpt as MAPF-OU, constructs contingent plans that branch on future observations, but its computational cost grows as uncertainty grows. MAPF with imperfect maps, or MAPF-IM, interleaves planning and execution and replans when uncertain parts are directly observed, but neither approach uses spatial dependence to infer unobserved traversability. Guidance graphs and related weighted-graph methods provide a practical way to influence routes generated by existing planners, although their weights ordinarily shape traffic rather than represent environmental uncertainty. Other related directions include the Canadian Traveller Problem, macro-actions combining movement and sensing, sparse probabilistic roadmaps, partially observable online planning, and single-agent belief updates over spatially correlated blockage states. The paper positions scalable use of such spatial beliefs together with explicit multi-agent conflict resolution as less explored.

Limitations of prior work

A classical MAPF instance consists of an undirected environment graph and a set of agents with fixed starts and goals. The true traversable graph is determined by a realization of initially unknown location states, while the planner has a distribution over possible realizations. A joint plan must avoid vertex and edge conflicts in addition to coping with the possibility that a planned location is blocked. The paper restricts attention to one-shot MAPF, fixed start and goal positions, initially unknown location states, and environments that remain fixed during execution. Its central problem is how to use local observations and spatial dependence to make better route choices before all relevant locations have been directly observed.

Technical highlights

The Gaussian Markov Random Field couples neighboring latent traversability scores, so an observation can change posterior probabilities at unobserved nearby locations. Gaussian Belief Propagation provides an approximate online inference mechanism rather than enumerating every hidden map and observation history. MAGIC turns beliefs into traversal costs that reflect both the chance that a transition remains available and the cost of taking an alternative route after failure. The resulting weighted graph is supplied to a standard MAPF planner, preserving the planner's role in resolving multi-agent conflicts. The stated local, noiseless observation model ensures that an uncertain destination is checked before an agent enters it.

Conclusion

The paper formulates Belief-Aware MAPF for fixed environments with initially uncertain traversability and introduces MAGIC for online shared belief inference. MAGIC combines a Gaussian Markov Random Field, Gaussian Belief Propagation, and detour-aware costs with standard MAPF planners. The benchmark results reported in the excerpt indicate lower executed cost on most evaluated instances, including the stated 96.3% figure, across multiple planner families and scales. The central conclusion is that spatially correlated local observations can be operationalized to anticipate hidden obstacles and improve execution. Extending the framework to lifelong tasks and changing traversability is left for future work.

Limitations and open problems

The method assumes that map discrepancies remain fixed and that the task is one-shot with fixed starts and goals. Its observation model is local and noiseless, with adjacent uncertain locations observed before entry. Gaussian Belief Propagation and repeated deterministic replanning are approximations and do not provide a complete solution to the underlying contingent decision problem. Performance can be harmed by an inaccurate prior, including a prior that makes useful passages appear too unlikely. Why it matters to MAPF researchers

The work addresses a practical gap between perfectly known-map MAPF and direct-observation replanning by using one observation to anticipate nearby unobserved obstacles. Its interface through weighted traversal costs makes the uncertainty model usable with several existing MAPF planner families instead of requiring a new collision-resolution algorithm for each one. Evaluation at scales up to 800 agents is relevant to warehouse and other shared-space deployments where rerouting can be expensive.
