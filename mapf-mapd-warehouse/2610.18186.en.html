In short: This paper proposes a reinforcement-learning policy for lifelong multi-agent path finding (LMAPF) that makes fast movement decisions under changing traffic and across different layouts. A shared spatial field, goal-conditioned route choice, and destination allocation are trained directly with throughput, and the reported policy transfers from small training maps to unseen larger layouts while achieving throughput comparable to or higher than the evaluated baselines with per-step execution below 10 milliseconds.

What is this paper about?

The setting is lifelong multi-agent path finding, in which agents receive a new goal whenever they complete the current one, so operation continues rather than ending after a single set of goals. At every step, many agents must choose movements that avoid collisions, while larger populations increase the cost of updating decisions and logistics or real-time applications impose tight time budgets. Reuse across facilities or game maps also requires handling different obstacle, passage, and intersection geometries. The paper therefore treats sustained goal-completion throughput and per-step computational cost as joint practical concerns.

What is new compared with prior work?

The central contribution is a policy that computes a shared spatial field from the full map and agent state once per timestep with a convolutional network, then reuses that field for each agent’s goal-conditioned route choice. Route choice reweights a soft-distance reference distribution with learned guidance, while a sparse destination-allocation mechanism adjusts conflicts when multiple agents propose moves toward the same cell. The complete policy is trained end to end with average-reward reinforcement learning using throughput as the reward, without planner demonstrations or a separate online path planner. One policy is trained for each of a structured and an unstructured layout class on mixtures of small maps, with intended reuse on larger unseen maps and new agent populations in the same class.

What is the key technical idea?

LMAPF is formulated as a continuing Markov decision process on a four-connected grid in which obstacle cells are not traversable. The state includes the map, agent positions, goal information, previous moves, elapsed-task counters, and task-stream state, while the action is a joint movement proposal for all agents. Movement follows POGEMA’s soft collision rules, which prevent agents from occupying the same cell or exchanging positions in one timestep. The reward is the number of goals completed at a timestep, so average-reward learning optimizes a sustained completion rate rather than a terminal objective.

How was it validated?

The evaluation measures throughput and per-step execution cost on unseen layouts and also examines transfer as the agent population changes. For the structured and unstructured policies, the experiments use the terminal averaged checkpoint from each of three training seeds, average cases within each seed, and then weight the seed means equally. Baselines include non-learned PIBT, RHCR, WPPL, LLLG, and TrafficFlow, as well as SILLM, OnlineGGO, and Follower. The instances, task streams, and rollout lengths follow the released evaluation protocol of SILLM, according to the supplied excerpt.

Detailed section summary

Background

LMAPF continues after a goal is completed: an agent receives another task and remains part of the traffic stream. Prior work includes windowed planning, priority inheritance and backtracking, large-neighborhood refinement, congestion-aware guide paths, and learned action preferences combined with planners. The paper positions itself against approaches that depend on future planning, planned demonstrations, or a separate planner by learning traffic guidance directly from throughput. Its target is a compact policy whose computation is shared across agents rather than a collection of independently growing parameters.

Limitations of prior work

Given a fixed map and agent population, the system must choose joint movements at every timestep, avoid collisions, and maximize the long-run average number of completed goals. Since a new task is assigned after each completion, there is no terminal configuration in which all goals have been finished. Large populations, changing traffic, and different obstacle arrangements make both coordination quality and computation difficult. The desired policy must therefore preserve throughput while remaining fast and transferable.

Technical highlights

The shared-spatial-field network is reported to contain 39,615 parameters, with six additional scalars in the route operator and allocation mechanism. Soft distances provide fixed geometric information by exponentially weighting walk lengths to a goal; they contain neither traffic information nor learned parameters. The route operator reweights this reference law at a learned temperature and obtains a first-action marginal from route continuations whose extent is bounded independently of map size. Sinkhorn-style allocation continuously adjusts competing destination demands inside the navigation policy.

Experiments

Training uses mixtures of small tiles and produces separate policies for structured and unstructured layout classes. The unstructured mixture contains random maps, dumbbell layouts, and street crops from Denver, Moscow, Boston, and Milan, while Berlin and Paris are excluded. The study evaluates transfer to larger maps outside the training pools, population changes, route-continuation depth, and retrained controls that remove destination allocation. The paper presents a throughput-trained reinforcement-learning policy for LMAPF that combines a shared spatial field with goal-conditioned route selection and differentiable destination allocation. Its main conclusion is that policies trained on mixtures of small maps can be reused on larger unseen maps and new populations without planner demonstrations, an online planner, or retraining for every layout. Why it matters to MAPF researchers

In large-scale LMAPF, the cost of updating every movement decision can determine whether a system is usable in real time, making shared computation an important design choice. Directly rewarding throughput lets the policy learn collective completion behavior under congestion rather than merely imitate individual routes. If the reported transfer is reproducible, small-map training could reduce the need for facility-specific planning or data collection. The approach is therefore relevant to both scalable learned coordination and comparisons between planning and policy execution costs.
