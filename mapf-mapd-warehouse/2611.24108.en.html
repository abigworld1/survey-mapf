Multi-Agent Path FindingMAPF
In short: Jin and colleagues study fleets with both robots on tasks and robots that have no current destination. Their FOCBS solver searches the paths of tasked robots and uses minimum-cost flow to move idle robots only as needed. Laboratory tests reported 40.8% less total fleet travel than a parking-based baseline.

What is this paper about?

The input is an undirected graph and distinct starting cells for all robots. Assigned robots have destinations, while unassigned robots may stop at any cell they can reach without conflict. The output must avoid vertex conflicts and opposite traversal of an edge. The primary objective is the sum of the times when assigned robots reach their destinations and remain there. For the selected assigned paths, the secondary objective is the total number of movements by unassigned robots.

What is new compared with prior work?

Treating idle robots as fixed obstacles can make an otherwise feasible task impossible, while directing them all to parking can add avoidable travel. FOCBS separates the search for individual destinations from routing robots with no destination. The important addition is that a failure of the flow solver produces constraints that guide the search toward other assigned paths. This lets the solver retain its primary optimality guarantee while accounting for the ability of idle robots to yield.

What is the key technical idea?

Conflict-based search first resolves collisions between assigned robots. For each mutually conflict-free set of assigned paths, the solver places those paths as reservations in a time-expanded network and finds an integral minimum-cost flow for the unassigned robots. Waiting costs nothing and moving incurs a cost. If insufficient flow can pass, the solver finds a minimum cut and identifies assigned reservations that block it. It branches by forbidding one blocker in each child and replans the affected assigned paths. A batched start for zero-cost routes reduces flow work, and tie-breaking among equal primary costs prefers paths that avoid idle robots’ starting cells.

How was it validated?

The benchmark compares FOCBS with a CBS variant that searches over all robots and with yielding adaptations of PIBT, MACGA, and LaCAM. The authors increased idle robot density in four standard maps, changed the assigned-to-idle ratio at fixed density, and removed key mechanisms in ablations. In the laboratory, three robots completed 40 task batches with no observed inter-robot collisions. Compared with sending idle robots to charging stations as parking, total travel fell 40.8%, idle-robot travel fell 80.5%, and average batch completion time fell 14.7%.

What are the limitations and open questions?

The proof guarantees termination and an optimal primary cost for feasible instances on finite graphs. It does not guarantee termination on infeasible instances. Idle movement is minimized for one selected primary-optimal set of assigned paths. Another set with the same primary cost could require fewer idle moves, so the result need not be globally lexicographically optimal. More assigned robots still increase the combinatorial search burden, and bottleneck maps can incur repeated flow diagnoses and network construction.

Detailed section summary

Background

A robot waiting for its next assignment can block a corridor used by another robot. A sensible response often requires moving only far enough to clear the corridor. This differs from standard MAPF, where each robot usually starts with a specified goal.

Limitations of prior work

Solving assigned paths first and then freezing them can leave idle robots with no feasible route. The obstruction may occur even when assigned robots do not collide with one another. Resolving such failures requires changing the assigned paths, rather than simply rerouting idle robots within the same reservations.

Technical highlights

Because idle robots have no distinct terminal destinations, the flow solver can route them as a single commodity. Vertex capacities and edge reservations encode collisions along the time horizon defined by the assigned robots’ final arrivals. The cut identifies a disjunction of reservations, at least one of which must change to enable a feasible completion.

Experiments

The single-threaded C++ benchmarks used an Intel Core i9-14900K and 64 GB of RAM. The usual time limit was 60 seconds. Solvers received identical seeded instances on open grids, random obstacles, bottleneck rooms, and warehouse aisles. The lab experiments evaluated mobile-base navigation between workstations on the same 65 navigation tasks for both methods.

Results

Increasing the number of idle robots at fixed assigned load favored the decomposition over searching on all robots. The branching ablation showed that simply discarding assigned paths that cannot be completed solves fewer instances. In the lab, assigned-robot travel also fell 20.2%, a result the authors associate with robots beginning later batches closer to workstations.

Conclusion

The main advantage comes from using the right representation for each group while feeding failures across the boundary. CBS retains control over assigned travel times, and network flow determines whether idle robots can yield economically. The lab result quantifies the benefit over one specific parking policy.

Limitations and open problems

The lab evaluation covers three mobile bases and excludes robotic arm manipulation. The episodic cost does not account for how a robot’s stopping position will affect future tasks. The authors identify alternative primary-optimal assigned paths, incremental network updates, and terminal-aware planning as future directions.

Why it matters to MAPF researchers

The study offers a way to exploit an asymmetry common in fleet coordination, where active tasks determine some destinations and leave others free. It also clarifies how a practical secondary objective can be optimized conditionally while preserving a provable primary objective.

Who should read this paper

Researchers in conflict-based search, time-expanded network flow, task-aware MAPF, or fleet parking policies will find the formulation useful. Readers designing persistent systems should pay particular attention to the difference between optimizing a single task batch and choosing terminal positions for future work.
