Multi-Agent Path FindingMAPF
In short: Tao and colleagues extend Priority Inheritance with Backtracking (PIBT) to robots that must turn in place before moving. By distinguishing a request for a neighboring cell from the action a robot can execute immediately, their solver retains fast one-step planning and improves execution in benchmark simulations and a small physical demonstration.

What is this paper about?

The input is an undirected grid, each robot’s starting cell and orientation, and one goal cell per robot. At each time step a robot can move forward, turn 90 degrees in place, or wait. The plan must avoid simultaneous occupancy of a cell and traversal of the same edge in opposite directions. The output is a sequence of actions for each robot, with no required orientation at the goal. The paper measures both the sum of individual completion times and the time until the last robot finishes.

What is new compared with prior work?

Classic PIBT resolves occupancy conflicts by having a blocking robot leave its cell on the next step. That assumption fails when the blocking robot must first change orientation. The authors preserve priority inheritance and its one-step planning structure while allowing a robot’s next action to differ from the move implied by its requested destination. They also distinguish a local deadlock from repeated pushing that keeps robots moving without making useful progress.

What is the key technical idea?

Robots propose next destinations in priority order. A blocking robot can inherit the requester’s priority and recursively request a destination of its own. The action update then determines whether a robot whose request succeeds can advance immediately or must remain in its cell and rotate. The planner handles circular chains of requests and uses an orientation-aware local selection rule. A swap-pattern detector addresses local deadlocks. A separate counter tracks repeated pushes between the same pair, triggering a randomized candidate order when the pattern persists so the lower-priority robot can explore a different route.

How was it validated?

The benchmark used five MovingAI maps and 25 cases for each tested agent count from 5 to 100 on each map, with a 60-second cutoff. The authors compared against an earlier turn-aware priority planner and an optimal turn-aware CBS solver. A separate warehouse stress test solved cases with up to 1,000 agents within seconds. In a simulated flexible manufacturing system, turn-aware planning reduced actual completion time by 23.3% for 10 agents and 20.2% for 20 agents compared with classical PIBT. In a three-TurtleBot4 demonstration, the turn-aware planner reached all three goals, while two robots deadlocked under classical PIBT with the same execution controller.

What are the limitations and open questions?

The planner works in an idealized discrete model, with fixed 90-degree rotations, specified start and goal cells, and one action per time step. Its one-step decisions prioritize speed and offer no global solution-quality guarantee in the reported evaluation. The repeated-push threshold trades earlier exploration against unnecessary deviations from individually short routes. The industrial layout study is simulated, and the hardware demonstration covers three robots on one obstacle map. Handling dynamic tasks, execution uncertainty, and online disturbances remains future work.

Detailed section summary

Background

Mobile robots need time to change direction and may continue to occupy a cell while turning. A plan built under instantaneous changes in direction can underestimate actual execution time or create poor coordination when translated into robot actions.

Limitations of prior work

Search-based solvers can represent orientation explicitly, but their search becomes more expensive as agent count grows. Classical PIBT offers lightweight coordination but couples destination reservation to immediate movement. That coupling is the specific obstacle to applying it to a model with rotations.

Technical highlights

A robot can request a neighboring cell before it is physically oriented to move into it. Separating the request from the next configuration prevents the planner from treating that robot’s current cell as immediately available. The repeated-push mechanism records which pairs repeatedly displace one another, so exploration is triggered by a persistent interaction.

Experiments

The C++ implementation ran on an Intel Core i5-12500 with 16 GB of RAM. The authors varied the push threshold and removed the escape and swap mechanisms in ablation tests. They also compared discrete plans through an action-dependency graph controller that converts planned moves into executable actions with ordering constraints.

Results

The warehouse stress test demonstrates scaling in a particular benchmark layout. The hardware experiment separates execution effects from controller choice, since both planners use the same controller. One robot reached its goal in 32 seconds with the proposed planner versus 41 seconds with classical PIBT, and the other two reached their goals at approximately 36 seconds with the proposed planner.

Conclusion

The study shows that orientation-aware planning can retain the speed of iterative priority inheritance while accounting for actions that occupy a cell without moving. An important lesson is that a plan’s discrete completion time and its measured execution time can rank planners differently.

Limitations and open problems

A low escape threshold may disrupt efficient paths in dense instances, while a high threshold lets repeated pushes continue. The experiments do not establish performance for arbitrary motion models or large real fleets. Benchmark scale and hardware scale should therefore be interpreted separately.

Why it matters to MAPF researchers

For MAPF research, the method identifies which assumption of classic priority inheritance breaks under realistic turning delays. Comparing theoretical and execution completion times also provides a concrete way to evaluate whether more accurate planning abstractions pay off in practice.

Who should read this paper

This paper is relevant to researchers adapting iterative MAPF to mobile robots, comparing orientation-aware planners, or evaluating how high-level plans translate into warehouse execution. Readers interested in formal guarantees should focus on how the discrete model, local decisions, and empirical evaluation delimit the claims.
